For two years a photograph of mine lived a life I knew nothing about.

I'd made it on a long-exposure pinhole — the Sound at Ribersborg gone to glass at dusk — and sent it off to [Flickr](https://www.flickr.com/photos/pjedlund/53236844443/) the way you drop a letter in a box and forget about it. And there, quietly, without me, people had been kind to it. Nine of them stopped long enough to say *yes, this one*. One — a stranger named Ludwig, whose face I will never see — left four words underneath: *Fine shot, perfectly realized.*

I never knew. And the one place that is actually mine — this site — knew nothing of it either. The conversation about my own picture was happening in a room I didn't own, and to everything I keep here, it was as if it had never happened at all.

[Webmentions](https://indieweb.org/Webmention) are the small, patient protocol that mends this — a way for one site to tap another on the shoulder and say *someone over here answered you.* Flickr doesn't speak it; almost no silo does. So you need a bridge, and the bridge is real work: a contraption of string and good intentions, confusing even when you know exactly what you're doing — the open web's own tools somehow clumsier than the walled gardens they're meant to free you from. I spent an afternoon coaxing two-year-old likes across it, one stubborn click at a time.

And then they arrived. A little row of faces along the foot of the page — most of them anonymous now, a couple still wearing their portraits. And beneath them, Ludwig's four words, at my address, in my type, two years late.

What stays with me isn't the plumbing. It's that the warm part of the thing — the part where someone you'll never meet looks at what you made and is moved enough to say so — had been kept somewhere else all this while, on loan from a company, returned only because I came and asked for it in the right awkward language. The IndieWeb isn't really about owning your words. It's about not letting the kindnesses land in someone else's house. They were always meant for me. It just took two years, and a rickety bridge, to bring them home.
