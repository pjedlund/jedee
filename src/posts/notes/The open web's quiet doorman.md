For the better part of a year I've been teaching this small site to speak the [indieweb](https://indieweb.org/)'s languages. To accept a [webmention](https://indieweb.org/Webmention), so a stranger's kindness can land at my own address. To take a post from my phone through a [Micropub](https://indieweb.org/Micropub) endpoint with the laptop shut. To pull a record or a film out of a browser tab and set it down here as a proper thing I own. It works now. And somewhere in the making of it, a small unease took up residence and hasn't left.

I believe every word of the ideals. Own your words. Own your web. Let the warm part of the thing — the *yes, this one* from someone you'll never meet — come home instead of living on loan from a company. I felt the truth of that first-hand the day two years of likes finally arrived at my page. The dream is good. I'm not arguing with the dream.

I'm arguing with the *door*. Because to do the simplest of these things — to let one site tap another on the shoulder — you have to become fluent in a stack of awkward protocols, stand up a server, and debug a bridge that even the people who built it find confusing. I'm a designer. I know my way around HTML and CSS, and it still cost me afternoons I won't get back. So I keep arriving at the obvious question: what about everyone else? The friend who just wants a page with their name on it. The person who has something to say and no wish to learn what a token endpoint is.

There's a movement here whose banner reads *for everyone*, and whose actual threshold is a level of technical fluency most people will never have, and shouldn't have to want. Not a velvet rope hung on purpose — nobody is standing at the entrance turning people away. It's quieter than that. The difficulty *is* the doorman. And the people already inside climbed past it so long ago they've stopped seeing it at all.

You can read it in the bookmarks I keep — developers, my own kind, frustrated that this is still so hard in 2026. When the builders find the building difficult, that isn't a personal failing to be coached away. It's a closed shop wearing an open sign. I love the indieweb's heart, and I've started to mistrust its manners: a club so sure of its own welcome that it never thought to check whether anyone could actually get in.

I don't want to leave. I want the club to notice it's a club. The web won't be *for everyone* until having a site of your own costs no more than having an opinion — until the protocols disappear into tools so plain that nobody has to know they're there. Until then, *for everyone* is a hope wearing the clothes of a description. And I'm writing this from inside, where the view of the door is clearest, and the quietest.
