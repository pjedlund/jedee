Today I gave a single photograph somewhere to live.

The picture is a 6×17 of Ribersborg at dusk — the lower pinhole, Ektar, sixty seconds of exposure that turned the Öresund to poured glass. On the page it's small, a 1400-pixel strip you take in at a glance. But the frame holds far more than that: nine and a half thousand pixels across, the grain, the silt, the seaweed, the little dark shapes in the shallows you don't notice until you're close.

So I built the machinery to let you get close. An object store on its own subdomain, a CDN in front of it, a lightbox that waits until you ask before it fetches the full eleven megabytes. Press once and the whole calm sea fills the screen; press again and you're at one to one, nearer the grain than I stood to the water that evening.

It's a strange amount of scaffolding for one image — DNS records, a bucket in Stockholm, a certificate, a small component that kept colliding with its own name until I untangled it. But that is the trade pinhole has always asked for: a great deal of patience for one quiet frame. The true scan, a hundred and forty-six megabytes of it, sits in the same bucket where almost no one will ever look. I keep it anyway. It's the truth of the picture, and the original deserves a home even if the home stays mostly dark.
